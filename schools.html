For teachers, administrators, and school boards

A practical digital-creation experience for the school day.

Build Day is designed as a structured introduction to game design, iteration, and digital making. This page gives schools the information they need to decide whether the format fits their students, schedule, and policies.

Educational value

Teach the process behind a digital experience.

Students are not just consuming a game. They are defining a goal, organizing a space, testing a system, and explaining a design decision. Those actions make the learning observable and discussable in a classroom.

  • Design thinking: identify a player, goal, challenge, and feedback loop.
  • Digital literacy: understand that interactive experiences are constructed systems.
  • Communication: describe choices, ask for feedback, and revise with purpose.
  • Agency: see themselves as capable of making, not only using, technology.
Light-lift planning

Fit it into a day that already works.

Schools can use the provided schedule and starter activity with little advance preparation. A teacher or facilitator mainly needs to share the time, confirm access to school-approved devices, and be present while students work. If your district has extra review steps, leave time for those.

School planning questions

Clear answers before you schedule it.

The format is designed to be straightforward for schools to host.

⌁
School day

One planned day

The sample format is built around one scheduled day, with short demonstrations, a build period, testing, and reflection.

$
Cost clarity

No hidden promise

This concept assumes existing school devices and free Roblox Studio. Confirm any local costs for devices, support, connectivity, or staffing before approval.

✓
Teacher readiness

Ready to facilitate

Teachers do not need to become Studio experts. The guided activity and quick-start materials do most of the explaining; adults mainly keep students moving and help with questions.

✓
Safety

Supervision stays local

Schools set the rules for accounts, devices, privacy, communication, and adult presence. Keep the activity inside approved school procedures.

↗
Evidence

Show the learning

Use a prototype, a short design explanation, and one revision as evidence of learning. A polished published game is not required.

★
Inclusion

No “winner” required

Offer starter templates, pairs, planning on paper, different sharing options, and credit for iteration so students do not have to compete to belong.

A realistic commitment

Guarantee the process, not an identical final product.

No honest program can guarantee that every student will leave with the same level of confidence or the same finished game. A stronger commitment is to give every student a supported entry point, a concrete build action, and a way to explain what they learned.

Suggested evidence of learning
  • 01
    One design decision“Players will ___ because ___.”
  • 02
    One playable changeA map, goal, interaction, or rule that can be tested.
  • 03
    One reflectionWhat worked, what changed, and what the student would try next.
For decision-makers

Questions to confirm locally.

A full training program is not the intent. A short orientation is recommended so teachers can open the project, understand the activity sequence, and know how to support students. Schools should decide what training their staff and policies require.
This concept is designed around existing school devices and free Roblox Studio, but local costs can still include devices, network support, staffing, or approved accounts. Confirm those items before scheduling.
Use starter templates, pairs, multiple ways to participate, and reflection-based sharing. Do not make publishing, speed, or competitive scores the definition of success.
